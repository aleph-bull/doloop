Why it works.

Every part of Komogon is there for a reason.

  1. Put yourself on camera., read the study

    Silvia & Duval, 2001 · Personality and Social Psychology Review

    In decades of experiments, people who could see themselves, in a mirror or on camera, held themselves to their own standards and tried harder to meet them. A timelapse keeps the camera on you for the whole session.

  2. Keep a record., read the study

    Harkin et al., 2016 · Psychological Bulletin

    Across 138 studies, people who tracked their progress were more likely to reach their goals, and more so when it was recorded or shared. A timelapse does both.

  3. Take breaks on a timer., read the study

    Biwer et al., 2023 · British Journal of Educational Psychology

    Students on timed Pomodoro breaks were less tired and more focused than those who stopped whenever they felt like it, and got the same work done in less time.

  4. Pick a day and a time., read the study

    Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006 · Advances in Experimental Social Psychology

    Across 94 studies, deciding exactly when and where you'll do something made people much more likely to do it. Plan my week finds each task a day and a time.

  5. Spread it out., read the study

    Cepeda et al., 2006 · Psychological Bulletin

    In a review of 317 experiments, material studied over several days was remembered better than the same time spent in one sitting. Plan my week spreads each task over the days before it's due.

  6. Know where you stand., read the study

    Locke & Latham, 2002 · American Psychologist

    Specific goals beat “do your best”, as long as you can see how you're doing against them. Analytics puts this week beside last week and your goal.

  7. Study alongside others., read the study

    Carr & Walton, 2014 · Journal of Experimental Social Psychology

    People who felt they were working with others stuck at a hard puzzle 48% longer, even though they were working alone. Groups and Live now show who's putting in the hours with you.

  8. Miss a day, keep the habit., read the study

    Lally et al., 2010 · European Journal of Social Psychology

    New habits took about two months to set in, and missing the odd day didn't hold people back. Days studied shows yours taking shape.
